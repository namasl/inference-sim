Does EDPP actually beat trivial heuristics?

A policy study of joint prefill/decode routing on a fixed disaggregated topology. This page reports what we ran, what we found, and — explicitly — what we have not yet established.

2026-07-17 branch feat/edpp-estimator-validation 4 merged plans · all reviewed every number reproducible

The bottom line

The shipped full rule is the best arm in 0 of 12 tested cells. Everywhere it is dominated by one of its own ablated subsets — strip a term out and the rule gets better. We now know term-by-term why, and every failure we found has a single cause.

The unifying finding — measured, not argued Every term in the rule prices the deciding request's own experience. The one term that prices the effect on others — the congestion drift — does it in the wrong currency: work, not value-at-risk.

Four independent experiments converge on that sentence — the decode-bound veto, the prefill-bound collapse, the Type-A/B workload failure, and the SLO-class backfire. There is a genuine positive: in the prefill-bound regime the optimum is an interior split and EDPP finds it dynamically (0.917 vs 0.604 for the best static plan). Ablation shows that win is delivered entirely by the congestion-drift term — the drift structure is the part worth keeping. The full apparatus around it is not.

How we got here

Ten beats, in order. Each one is the reason the next one happened. Written to be read aloud.

01

The policy wasn't beating the simple alternatives

We built EDPP and tested it on identical hardware. It didn't beat “always disaggregate.” Our read was that a smart policy should earn its keep when the hardware is unequal, because knowing one server is faster is exactly the thing a naive rule cannot know.

So: go build hardware heterogeneity and test there.

02

We couldn't test that, so we built it first

The simulator could describe per-GPU hardware but never actually applied it. The setting was documented and never wired up. We wired it, and confirmed two decode servers now run at genuinely different speeds — a 4.3× latency gap between them.

Delivered: the first of four merged, reviewed code changes.

03

First real test: there was headroom, and EDPP took it

With one fast and one slow server, the best possible routing scored 1.0. The best hardware-blind policy managed 0.77. EDPP got 0.97. But the test was too easy. The fast server could serve everything, so “use the fast one” was the whole answer.

So: make it harder, where the answer isn't trivial.

04

We made it harder, and EDPP fell behind

We saturated the fast server, so the best split was about 86 percent to the fast one and 14 to the slow. Every policy, EDPP included, settled at 77 percent and left goodput on the table. They all balance queues. The optimum deliberately overloads the fast server, because it drains quicker.

So: give EDPP the hardware model itself, so it can weigh the fast server properly.

05

That made it worse

With per-instance hardware coefficients, EDPP over-corrected. It shoved 95 to 97 percent onto the fast server and goodput collapsed below the policies it was supposed to beat. We had added fidelity and lost performance.

So: stop adding machinery. Something upstream is wrong.

06

We stopped and asked what EDPP is actually optimizing

The answer was uncomfortable. Its SLO targets are constraints, not goals. The only thing it minimizes is the cost of moving the KV cache. Under overload those constraints can't be met, so that objective is meaningless — you're missing deadlines either way. Worse, its internal queues accumulate lateness, so it keeps spending capacity on requests that already missed and can never count. Goodput doesn't work like that. Once a request misses, more effort on it is worth zero.

So: the question isn't “which term do we add.” It's whether routing is the lever at all.

07

We mapped where routing even matters

On identical hardware with identical requests, it doesn't. The naive corner is already optimal and no policy can beat it. Along the way we found something separate and useful — llm-d's shipped router pins every decode request onto a single server, costing 6.6× goodput. That's a finding about a production default, not about our policy.

So: sweep the whole workload spectrum and find the regime where routing is real.

08

We found where EDPP genuinely wins — then found it wasn't the machinery

Across the spectrum, the winning naive rule flips. On prefill-heavy overload the best split is an interior one, and EDPP finds it dynamically — 0.917 against 0.604 for the best fixed plan. That's real and it holds across seeds. Then we built a one-line baseline — disaggregate if the predicted latency is lower, with none of EDPP's machinery — and it captures most of the win, and under extreme overload it appears to beat EDPP six to one — though we later trace most of that gap to a transfer-cost artifact, not real robustness (see “Two confounds we ruled out”).

So: don't ask “is EDPP better” — ask which term is doing the work. Turn them off one at a time.

09

We ablated the rule term by term — and found the currency error

The full rule wins in none of twelve cells. The congestion-drift term alone delivers the whole prefill-bound win — and also causes the overload collapse. The SLO virtual queues (z) help a little on prefill-heavy work but hurt decode-bound badly: they price disaggregation's cost while blind to the decode capacity it buys, so they veto the right answer. And the transfer cost — the one thing the rule officially minimizes — is four orders of magnitude too small to move any decision.

The pattern: every term prices the deciding request's own experience. The one externality term prices it in work, not value. So under overload it balances two hopeless queues instead of protecting the requests still in reach.

10

We turned on the two things we'd never tested — both failed the same way

SLO classes were supposed to be EDPP's home turf, the one axis a class-blind rule can't touch. Giving EDPP per-class targets made critical goodput worse: deprioritising a class doesn't make it yield capacity, it makes it selfish. And joint routing — the project's central thesis, EDPP choosing the decode instance itself instead of the scorer — was a wash. It fixed none of the failures. Handed control of the decode choice on the mixed workload, the work-currency drift routed worse than plain load balancing.

Where that leaves us: three heterogeneity axes tested, all three failed for the same reason. The formulation's coupling argument is real but second-order. One idea remains untested — change what the drift term measures — and one honest experiment can settle it.

Why we built a joint rule at all

The design starts from one claim: routing and deciding are the same problem, and the system currently splits them. Today two mechanisms run in sequence — a scorer picks the decode instance, then a separate decider chooses whether to prefill locally or disaggregate. The formulation folds them into a single action.

ar = ( dr , pr )    where   pr ∈ prefill-nodes ∪ { local } “local” is just another prefill location — so “disaggregate?” becomes a coordinate of “which instance?”

The split is lossy because the two decisions are coupled, in both directions:

  • The value of disaggregating depends on which decode instance you picked. Offloading prefill helps a congested decode instance far more than an idle one.
  • The best decode instance depends on whether you will disaggregate. If you will, pick on decode capacity alone — that instance never prefills. If you won't, pick on combined prefill and decode capacity. Those can be different instances.

So a decomposed pipeline picks the instance blind to the split, then picks the split blind to the instances it could have chosen. The joint rule evaluates every (instance, split) pair together. Because it optimizes over a superset of the same objective, it is provably no worse than the reduced form; what it costs is compute and llm-d scorer parity.

Formulation §5.5 — on the reduced rule we actually shipped “Fixing d externally is exactly the loss the joint formulation removes… the pairwise rule cannot see that a different decode node would have made disaggregation unnecessary.” The reduction is “a deployment choice…, not a mathematical necessity.”

The uncomfortable consequence — and how it resolved. For most of this study every experiment used the reduced rule — the scorer-chosen slice — measuring the very decomposition the formulation was written to replace. That was the study's biggest gap, so we finally switched the joint mechanism on and re-ran the ablation. The result is below: joint routing is a wash. The coupling this section argues for is real, but second-order — it is not what is holding EDPP back.

What EDPP optimizes

EDPP is a Lyapunov drift-plus-penalty policy. In that framework the penalty is the objective you minimize, and constraints are enforced by virtual queues that accumulate violation. Which is which matters enormously, and it is easy to get backwards. The formulation states the problem the rule is derived from:

Formulation §5.1 — the problem the decision rule minimizes “Write g(t) for the operating cost we would rather avoid — here the transfer / KV-movement cost incurred by disaggregation… minimize its time average subject to stability and the SLO constraints.”

So the SLO targets are constraints, carried as virtual queues. The single thing being minimized is the transfer cost. Its three terms:

TermWhat it isKind
qi · Δworkiper-instance backlogdrift — enforces throughput stability
zttft · T̂/τ , zitl · (…)/τvirtual queues for the TTFT / ITL SLOsdrift — enforces the SLO constraints
V · cxferthe KV-transfer costpenalty — the only thing minimized

Read plainly, the implemented rule's posture is: “disaggregate as little as possible, subject to holding the SLOs and keeping the queues stable.”

That is coherent when the SLOs are feasible. Under overload the constraints are infeasible — no policy can hold them, the virtual queues grow without bound, the theoretical guarantee is void, and “minimize transfers” is beside the point because you are missing the deadline either way. Worse, the queues accumulate lateness, so the policy keeps spending capacity on requests that already missed and can never count toward goodput. Goodput saturates; this surrogate does not, so it cannot triage — it keeps rewarding progress on hopeless requests instead of protecting the ones still in reach.

This is our leading explanation for the collapse in Figure 3. The candidate correction is a saturating, goodput-faithful objective — a value that is flat-high before the deadline and stops decaying after — rather than another term bolted onto the rule. We have not done this. Every number on this page uses the implemented transfer-cost objective.

How we measure a policy

Goodput — the fraction of completed requests meeting all their SLO dimensions — because it is what an operator is graded on. We compare five policies on a fixed 1P2D topology (one prefill server, two decode servers). The operator picks the topology; the policy only routes. Comparing across topologies would answer a provisioning question instead.

The SLO is set per workload from an idle probe, so goodput is comparable across archetypes with very different intrinsic latency. Concurrency is capped to force saturation at reachable rates — with the default cap nothing queues and every policy scores a meaningless 1.000.

The two axes we had never tested

SLO classes and joint routing were the study's two biggest open gaps — and the two most likely to rescue EDPP. Both are now measured. Both failed the same way.

Per-class targets made critical goodput worse (0.859 → 0.814). The machinery did exactly what it was designed to do — batch's SLO queue went to exactly zero, prefill-server access shifted from batch (55%→29%) to critical (27%→43%) — and critical still lost, while the deprioritised class got faster (e2e 3424→1879 ms). Two confounds we ruled out

Before trusting the diagnosis, we checked two things a skeptic could blame instead of the currency: bad output-length estimates, and a mispriced transfer cost. Neither rescues EDPP — and the second retires an earlier claim of our own.

Output-length oracle — not the cause

We fed the rule each request's true output length instead of the running estimate (--edpp-oracle-output-len, a diagnostic upper bound). It's a near-no-op: the prefill-bound collapse is byte-identical and the decode-bound veto doesn't move. The reason is that every archetype here has a constant output length, so the estimate already converges to the truth — there's little error to remove. So output-length estimation is not what breaks EDPP (a variable-output workload would be needed to stress that dimension at all).

Transfer cost — a real bug in our own baseline

The decider assumed a flat 5 ms transfer cost, but the simulator actually executes a size-based KV transfer (∝ prefill tokens) and charges it to the request's TTFT. Measured, the flat constant is only coincidentally right for one archetype:

Correcting it retires our own “5–6×” claim. With the true transfer cost, least-ttft's apparent robustness under prefill-bound overload collapses (0.397 → 0.101) — it was under-charging transfers and over-disaggregating by luck. But the core diagnosis is untouched: drift-only is invariant (it never reads c_xfer), the full rule still wins 0 of 12, and the decode veto survives the correct smaller cost. The twist that reinforces the currency finding: giving least-ttft the true cost makes it more accurate about its own TTFT and therefore more globally wrong — it declines the disaggregation that would have helped the system.

The llm-d scorer finding

Findings

  1. llm-d's shipped decode scorer pins all decode traffic onto one instance under a shared prefix group, costing 6.6× goodput.
  2. The optimal naive corner flips across the spectrum — always for decode-bound, never for prefill-bound. No fixed heuristic is universally right.
  3. On prefill-bound overload the optimum is an interior split (f=35 → 0.604), beating both corners. Routing genuinely matters here.
  4. EDPP wins big in that regime and it is robust — 0.917 / 0.917 / 0.942 across seeds, beating both corners and the best static split by ~0.32. A genuine positive result.
  5. The shipped full rule is the best arm in 0 of 12 cells — dominated everywhere by one of its own ablated subsets, on both the reduced and joint paths.
  6. The congestion-drift term delivers the entire win — and causes the collapse. Best in 5 of 8 informative cells, yet the worst arm at prefill-bound rate ≥12 (0.264 vs least-ttft 0.492). One term, both faces.
  7. The SLO virtual queue (z) flips sign by archetype — hurts decode-bound by −0.110, helps prefill-heavy by ~+0.03. It prices disaggregation's TTFT cost while blind to the decode capacity it buys, so on decode-bound it vetoes the right answer and EDPP degenerates to never.
  8. The transfer penalty — the stated objective — is numerically invisible (max 0.0008 vs a TTFT term of 25.7). Removing it slightly helps.
  9. Per-class SLO machinery is counterproductive (critical goodput 0.859 → 0.814). Deprioritising a class doesn't make it yield capacity — it makes it selfish.
  10. Joint routing does not rescue EDPP — a wash that fixes none of the failures. The formulation's coupling argument is real but second-order.
  11. The unifying finding: every term prices the deciding request's own experience; the one externality term prices it in work, not value-at-risk. At moderate load work ≈ value, so drift wins; under overload it balances two hopeless queues instead of protecting the savable. One sentence accounts for F6, F7, F9, F10 and the Type-A/B failure.
  12. Output-length estimation is not the cause. An oracle that feeds the rule the true output length is a near-no-op — the collapse and veto survive. (These constant-output workloads can't stress the estimate; that dimension is still untested on variable output.)
  13. least-ttft's overload robustness was a transfer-cost artifact. The decider under-charged transfers (flat 5 ms vs the real ~53 ms); priced correctly, least-ttft collapses too (0.397 → 0.101). drift-only is invariant, the full rule still wins 0/12, and the decode veto is not a transfer-cost artifact.

(Separately, giving EDPP the hardware model itself — per-instance coefficients — made it worse: it over-corrects, overloading the fast node past the optimum. See beat 05.)

What we have not established

Two of the gaps from the earlier version of this page are now closed by measurement, not argument: joint routing was exercised (a wash) and SLO-class heterogeneity was tested (counterproductive). What still bounds every claim above:

01The value-currency fix is untested. Every finding points at it — price value-at-risk instead of work — but nothing has measured it. This is the one live idea left, and the honest next experiment.
02The objective was never changed. The rule still minimizes transfer cost subject to the SLO constraints — a poor surrogate for goodput once those constraints go infeasible. Every number on this page uses the shipped objective.
03The ITL virtual queue never fired. Measured inter-token latency never approached its target, so half the SLO machinery has been dead throughout — the ablation is of the TTFT queue specifically.
04One topology (1P2D), cap 16. The concurrency cap is an artificial stressor; without it nothing saturates and every policy scores 1.000. Joint's action set here is only four candidates — a wider pool might give it more to work with.
05The yardstick is a static fraction sweep, not the true joint hindsight optimum (a MILP, still unbuilt). Most cells are 3 seeds; the prefill-bound rate-12 collapse is bimodal across seeds.

What we recommend next

There is exactly one idea left that could rescue the design: change what the drift term measures. Keep the drift structure — a virtual queue can measure anything — but price value-at-risk (the marginal goodput destroyed by adding work at an instance) instead of raw work. It is computable, and it addresses the collapse, the class backfire, and the Type-A/B failure at once, because all three are the same missing externality.

  • Test the ceiling before paying for the machinery — an oracle upper bound. Read the true output length rather than estimating it (an explicitly oracle-marked, diagnostic-only mode; every number reported as an upper bound, never an achievable policy). If it fails with a perfect oracle, the idea is dead and we've saved the estimator work.
  • Value-at-risk drift plus a saturating utility, measured against the fixed-plan yardstick across the same archetypes.

The trap to watch for. A saturating utility gives ≈ 0 for doomed requests — which is no signal, so their placement falls to the remaining terms and they grab the cheapest contended resource, reproducing the class failure exactly. Saturation alone gives neglect, not triage. Triage needs the doomed to yield capacity — an admission/scheduling lever EDPP does not hold. That is a real boundary on the whole approach, and it should be stated up front, not discovered late.

The work supports a characterization paper today, and a stronger one than “our policy wins by X%”: which heterogeneity axes justify adaptive P/D routing, why a principled drift-plus-penalty policy is dominated by its own ablated subsets, and the single currency error that explains all four failures — plus the llm-d scorer finding, which is independently useful. Whether it also becomes a method paper depends entirely on the oracle experiment above.
